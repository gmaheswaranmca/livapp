Yes, **some participants will test you**, but probably not in the way you're imagining.

In a mixed-department audience, you'll typically see a few different types of participants:

1. **The experienced developer** – asks deep technical questions.
2. **The beginner** – asks basic questions.
3. **The silent observer** – listens and rarely speaks.
4. **The challenger** – asks questions to assess your knowledge.
5. **The practical learner** – wants to know how this applies to their work.

The first and fourth groups are the ones you're concerned about.

### How to handle experienced developers

If someone asks:

> "Why not use multiprocessing instead of threading?"

That's a good question. You can answer:

> "Excellent question. Multiprocessing is better for CPU-bound work because it avoids the GIL. Today's course focuses on I/O-bound concurrency using threading and asyncio, which matches the course objectives."

That shows you know the broader picture without going off-topic.

---

If someone asks:

> "Why not use FastAPI instead of sockets?"

You can say:

> "FastAPI is built on networking concepts and uses asyncio internally. Today we're learning the underlying socket and concurrency concepts before moving to higher-level frameworks."

Again, you're connecting the question back to the course.

---

### What if you don't know an answer?

Never guess.

Instead, say something like:

> "I haven't verified that behavior recently. Let's note it down, and I'll confirm it after the session."

Experienced professionals respect honesty much more than an incorrect answer.

---

### Stay aligned with the course scope

If someone asks about topics outside the agenda—for example, `multiprocessing`, `uvloop`, or advanced network protocols—you can respond:

> "That's a useful topic, but it's outside today's scope. We can discuss it after the session if time permits."

This keeps the class on schedule.

---

### Remember why you're there

The company hired you to teach **this course**, not to prove that you know every feature of Python.

If you can clearly explain:

* `argparse`
* `subprocess`
* `socket`
* `threading`
* `queue`
* `asyncio`

with working demonstrations and practical advice, you've met the objectives.

---

### Prepare for likely questions

Spend a little time reviewing topics that commonly come up:

* What is the GIL?
* Threading vs multiprocessing
* Threading vs asyncio
* Blocking vs non-blocking I/O
* `subprocess.run()` vs `subprocess.Popen()`
* TCP vs UDP (high level)
* Daemon vs non-daemon threads
* When to use `queue.Queue`
* Why use `await`?
* What is an event loop?

If you're comfortable answering these, you'll handle the vast majority of technical questions related to this course.

### One final point

From what I've seen in our conversations, you've taught technologies like .NET, Angular, AWS, Docker, databases, and data structures. That experience matters. This course is about **explaining and demonstrating practical Python modules**, not inventing new concurrency models or discussing Python internals in depth.

If you prepare your demos, understand the "why" behind each module, and stay within the advertised scope, you'll be in a strong position even with a technically diverse audience.
